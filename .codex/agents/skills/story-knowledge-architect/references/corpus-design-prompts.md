# Corpus Design Prompts

Use these patterns when creating research prompts for `king-research`.

## Prompt Shape

Good prompts are narrative-oriented:

```text
Research <topic> for a fiction project about <premise>. Focus on facts, constraints, routines, conflicts, vocabulary, and scene-useful details. Prioritize what affects character decisions, plausibility, social consequences, and plot opportunities. Avoid generic encyclopedia summary. Include common misconceptions and anachronism risks when relevant.
```

## Depth Guidance

### Low

Use for texture and narrow details.

```text
Research <narrow topic> for quick scene texture in <work premise>. Focus on concrete details a writer can use in description, dialogue, objects, gestures, spaces, and daily routine. Keep scope narrow and avoid broad history unless it changes scene behavior.
```

### Low: History Of A Featured Invention

Use when a specific invention, object, instrument, tool, book, weapon, machine, food, medicine, or craft process becomes a chapter centerpiece or arc milestone.

```text
Research the history of <invention/object> for a fiction project about <work premise>. Focus on origins, early uses, adoption path, social or political impact, cultural meaning, misconceptions, named historical examples, and scene hooks. Keep technical construction details brief unless they affect adoption or symbolism. Prioritize what helps a writer dramatize why this invention mattered.
```

### Medium

Use for recurring domains.

```text
Research <domain> for a recurring fiction project need in <work premise>. Focus on practical constraints, institutions, workflows, vocabulary, common conflicts, and narrative consequences across multiple scenes. Include what a writer must know to avoid shallow or implausible scenes.
```

### High

Use for structural engines.

```text
Research <core domain> as a structural engine for <work premise>. Map baseline conditions, key constraints, plausible change paths, dependencies, failure modes, social/political/economic consequences, and story hooks. Highlight what must be decided before drafting and what can remain flexible.
```

## Delta-Specific Patterns

### Transferred Expertise

```text
Research the knowledge bridge for <expert/protagonist origin> applying <expertise> in <destination world/time/place>. Compare what the protagonist may know with the materials, tools, institutions, and constraints available in the destination. Focus on plausible adaptations, impossible leaps, intermediate steps, and consequences.
```

### Historical Immersion

```text
Research <period/place/community> for historical fiction. Focus on governance, social hierarchy, daily life, material culture, beliefs, language/register, conflict sources, and anachronism risks that would affect scenes and character choices.
```

### Professional Realism

```text
Research <profession/institution> for fiction. Focus on workflow, hierarchy, jargon that can be used sparingly, ethical constraints, common errors in fiction, conflict points, and scene rhythms.
```

### Invented System Support

```text
Research real-world analogues and fiction design patterns for <invented system>. Focus on limits, costs, loopholes, training, institutions, economy, warfare, religion, and how rules create plot rather than solve every problem.
```

### Speculative Consequence

```text
Research consequences of introducing <change/technology/rule/event> into <baseline world>. Focus on first-order and second-order effects, winners and losers, resistance, resource bottlenecks, institutional response, and story conflicts.
```

## Success Criteria

For each corpus, define success criteria before running research:

```text
- Gives scene-useful details, not only abstract background.
- Identifies constraints and failure modes.
- Names anachronism or plausibility risks.
- Produces conflict hooks and consequence chains.
- Separates known facts from speculation.
```
